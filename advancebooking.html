Building a SaaS business to understand one

A live multi-tenant booking and point-of-sale platform with subscription billing on Stripe — where pricing, packaging and merchant onboarding stop being abstractions.

Product
AdvanceBooking
My role
Sole designer, builder and product owner
Period
Jul 2025 – present
Status
Live at advancebooking.app, pilot users

Why this exists

I market software to businesses. It seemed unreasonable to keep doing that without having once been on the other side of the decisions — what to charge, what to put behind which plan, what a merchant will tolerate during onboarding, and what happens operationally when a subscription payment fails at two in the morning.

AdvanceBooking is a multi-tenant SaaS booking and point-of-sale platform for service businesses. Merchants manage staff, services, bookings, customers, memberships and billing from one dashboard; their customers book through a public widget. It runs in production.

This is not an engineering credential and I am not applying for engineering roles. Its value to a marketing role is that it makes my understanding of SaaS business models, subscription payments and merchant needs concrete rather than theoretical — and lets me hold a substantive conversation with a product team.

The customer problem

Small service businesses — salons, clinics, studios — run on a patchwork of phone bookings, messaging apps, paper diaries and a card terminal. The failure is not any single tool; it is that the customer record, the booking, the payment and the loyalty balance live in four places that never reconcile. The operator becomes the integration layer, and does that work during the hours they should be serving customers.

Commercial decisions

  • Pricing and packaging. Three published tiers — Solo, Team and Business at SGD 39, 99 and 199 a month — plus an enterprise tier, with feature gating and plan entitlements behind them. The genuinely difficult decision was which features gate upgrades without making the entry tier useless. Staff seats turned out to be the honest boundary, because they track the value a business is actually getting rather than an arbitrary usage cap.
  • Trial over freemium. A 14-day trial with no card required, rather than a permanent free plan. For a product that replaces an operator's diary, the barrier is switching effort, not price — so the trial has to prove the switch is survivable. A free tier that never binds produces no revenue; one too limited to run a real week produces no conviction.
  • Time-to-value over feature depth. A new merchant is provisioned with working defaults at signup rather than an empty dashboard. Repeatedly, the trade-off was fewer options in exchange for a merchant reaching their first real booking sooner.
  • Building for the operator, not the buyer. The people using this all day are front-desk staff, so permissions, quick actions and error recovery mattered more than dashboards for the owner.

Payments and billing

Subscription billing runs on Stripe. Designing it end to end meant working through the parts that are invisible until they break: the subscription lifecycle across upgrades, downgrades, failed payments and cancellations; what a merchant should still be able to do while payment is failing; how entitlements resolve when a plan changes mid-cycle; and reconciling webhook events with application state so the two never silently diverge.

Alongside subscriptions, the commerce side handles the messy reality of how service businesses actually charge: checkout with add-ons and products, credits and prepaid packages, memberships and their benefits, discounts, gift cards, and refunds and voided transactions that must return credits correctly. Most of the difficulty in payments is not taking money — it is everything that happens afterwards.

Production discipline

Because it is live and holds other businesses' customer data, it is built to be operated rather than demonstrated: tenant isolation enforced at the database level through row-level security; multi-factor authentication with email-token gating on sensitive actions; audit logging on destructive operations; automated unit, integration and end-to-end test suites; error monitoring; and continuous deployment.

How AI changed the work

I built and shipped this as a solo operator using AI-assisted development throughout. The lesson that transferred directly to marketing was that AI collapses the cost of producing a first version, which moves the bottleneck to judgement: deciding what should exist, what good looks like, and what to reject. The volume of output stopped being the constraint. Knowing which output was worth keeping became the constraint.

That is now how I work on marketing production too — generate broadly, then apply strict editorial judgement, rather than treating the draft as the finish line.

Status and honesty about scale

The platform is live and running with pilot users. I am not going to describe it as a successful business until it has paying customers who would say so. What it demonstrably is: a complete, production-grade product built end to end by one person, in front of real operators, and the reason I can talk about SaaS pricing, subscription payments and merchant onboarding from experience.

Evidence

Publicly verifiable: advancebooking.app (opens in a new tab) — the live product, pricing and public booking flow.

What I learned

  • Pricing is positioning. Every gating decision is a statement about who the product is for. I had written positioning documents for years before understanding that the pricing page is the positioning.
  • Onboarding is where marketing promises get tested. The gap between what a landing page implies and what the first ten minutes deliver is where trust is lost.
  • Payments failure states are a product surface. How a platform behaves when a card is declined shapes merchant trust more than any feature.
  • Constraints clarify. Being the only person meant every feature had a real cost, which made prioritisation honest in a way it rarely is with a roadmap.
