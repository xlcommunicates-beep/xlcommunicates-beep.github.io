When the licence arrives before the brand does

Repositioning a digital-asset firm that had become a regulated fund manager — for allocators who decide on fiduciary credibility, not technology.

Client
TradeTogether, Singapore
My role
Independent brand consultant (freelance)
Status
Live at tradetogether.com (2026)
Audience
Family offices, private banks, accredited investors

Business context

TradeTogether was founded in Singapore in 2021 to bring risk discipline to digital-asset investing. Over four years it did something most firms in that category never do: it became regulated. By 2026 it held a Capital Markets Services licence and operated through a Variable Capital Company structure, with the governance and compliance apparatus that implies.

Its target client had changed accordingly — from individuals interested in digital assets to family offices, external asset managers and accredited investors allocating meaningful capital.

The problem

The firm's regulatory and operational maturity had outpaced its brand. Everything about how it presented itself — visual language, tone, the story it told about its own origins, the structure of its website — still communicated digital-asset platform.

That is a trust gap, and in this audience it is decisive. A family office allocating to a fund manager is not buying technology. It is buying fiduciary credibility: who is regulated, who is accountable, where the assets sit, what happens if something fails. A brand that reads as a crypto platform invites exactly the wrong first question, and the conversation is often over before the fund's actual merits are ever discussed.

The firm had done the hard institutional work. Its brand was still arguing for a different company.

Strategic objective

Close the gap between what the firm had become and what it appeared to be — so that the brand stopped being an obstacle in front of sophisticated allocators. Explicitly not a rebrand: the name, the Singapore roots and the risk-first origins were to survive intact.

My precise role

Engaged as an independent consultant to diagnose and recommend, not to decide. I ran the brand audit, defined the positioning and identity, developed the narrative and tone of voice, produced the visual identity and full brand guidelines, and designed the website content architecture. Every strategic call was the client management's to make; my job was to give them a defensible basis for making it.

Stakeholders and constraints

  • Founder and management — who had built the firm's story personally, which made parts of the diagnosis uncomfortable to deliver.
  • A regulated perimeter. Positioning for a licensed fund manager cannot outrun what the firm is permitted to say. Claims have to survive scrutiny, not just resonate.
  • Continuity as a hard requirement. "Elevate, do not rebrand" was the brief. Four years of equity had to be preserved.
  • An audience that is simultaneously alpha-hungry and risk-averse — the positioning had to satisfy both without leaning into either.

Decisions and trade-offs

Define the brand by what it is not. The most useful section of the audit was an explicit two-column statement: what the firm is, and what it is not. For a business exiting a category, negative definition does more work than positive definition — it is far easier to align a team on what to stop sounding like. The trade-off is that telling a client their current identity is actively costing them credibility is uncomfortable, and risks the engagement. I judged that a diagnosis that avoided it would have been worthless.

Quiet authority over distinctiveness. In a category where nearly everyone competes on visual energy, I recommended the opposite: the restraint and density of a conservative financial institution. This is a real trade-off — the brand becomes less immediately distinctive in exchange for being more immediately credible. For an audience whose first instinct is due diligence, credibility is the scarcer asset.

Keep the name despite a real liability. The audit identified a perception problem with the firm's own origin story. I recommended keeping the name and changing the story around it rather than discarding four years of recognition. That leaves a known weakness in place; I thought the continuity was worth more, and said so explicitly rather than burying the finding.

Structure before narrative on the website. The site was restructured to lead with fund structure, governance and regulatory standing — the things this audience checks first — rather than with the technology story the firm found more interesting to tell.

Approach and execution

  • Brand audit. Diagnosed the perception gap, mapped the competitive landscape and positioned the firm within it, and set out a forward roadmap.
  • Identity definition. The is / is-not framework, written to be usable by anyone in the firm as a filter for future decisions.
  • Narrative architecture. Rebuilt the story from purpose through method to product, so the reasoning arrived before the offer.
  • Tone of voice. Defined with concrete does and don'ts — paired examples of acceptable and unacceptable phrasing, so the standard survives without me.
  • Visual identity. Developed distinct creative directions for management to choose between, rather than presenting a single option as a fait accompli.
  • Full brand guidelines covering logo system, colour, type, usage rules and application across collateral.
  • Website content architecture and design, ordered around what the audience needs to verify rather than what the firm wants to say.

Outcome

Delivered as a complete, self-sufficient brand system: audit and roadmap, identity definition, narrative and tone of voice, visual identity and logo system, full brand guidelines, collateral templates, and website content architecture and design.

The repositioning is now live, and the recommendations are visible in what shipped:

  • The firm introduces itself as a licensed fund management company regulated by the Monetary Authority of Singapore, licence number stated — regulated standing first, product second.
  • Navigation leads with strategies and funds rather than a technology narrative, following the recommended content order.
  • A dedicated governance section sets out the VCC structure, legal segregation and independent oversight — the anchor the audit argued this audience verifies before anything else.
  • An accredited-investor eligibility gate makes the regulatory perimeter explicit at entry rather than burying it in a footer.
  • The visual and verbal register is engineering and infrastructure, not crypto — the language of a financial engineer rather than a trader, as set out in the tone of voice.

What I will not claim is a commercial outcome. Whether the repositioning changes how allocators actually respond is not something I have visibility into, and a brand consultant taking credit for a fund's inflows would be precisely the kind of unfounded claim this project existed to design out.

Evidence

What I took from it

The hard part of repositioning is rarely the new identity. It is persuading people that something they built deliberately, and are proud of, has quietly started working against them. The audit only worked because it was specific about why each element was now a liability for this particular audience — general assertions that a brand "feels startup-y" would have been dismissed, correctly.

The second lesson transfers directly to institutional payments, which is where I work now: in regulated finance, restraint is not the absence of a creative position. It is the position. Audiences who allocate capital read visual and verbal excess as a risk signal, and the discipline to say less is what earns the meeting.
