An early-stage company at a summit full of louder ones

Owning a conference programme end to end on a small budget — and finding that its real return was a demand signal we had not gone looking for.

Organisation
Teleskop Technologies
My role
Marketing Manager — programme owner
Event
Echelon Asia Summit 2025, Suntec Singapore, 10–11 June 2025
Footprint
Marketplace booth M04, 3m × 3m

Business context

Teleskop is an early-stage fintech working in digital legacy planning and financial wellness — a category most people have never consciously considered and actively avoid thinking about. Echelon Asia Summit is one of Southeast Asia's larger startup and technology gatherings, drawing founders, investors, corporates and press across two days.

The problem

An early-stage company at a summit like this is structurally disadvantaged. It has a 3×3 metre booth next to companies with far more budget and recognition, an unfamiliar product category, and roughly four seconds to make a passer-by stop. Attending without a clear purpose is a common and expensive way to feel busy.

Strategic objective

Rather than treating the event as general awareness, define a narrow purpose: make the category legible to a relevant subset of attendees, and generate a small number of conversations with genuine potential — partners, distribution channels and informed early users — rather than maximising badge scans.

My precise role

I owned this programme end to end. Objectives, audience definition, booth narrative and creative, vendor selection and contracts, compliance and technical submissions, on-site execution and follow-up. I was the named company signatory on the exhibitor agreements. This is the clearest example I have of full programme ownership, albeit at small scale.

Stakeholders

Founders (positioning and commercial priorities); event organiser and appointed event manager (deadlines, approvals, technical compliance); external print, fabrication and AV vendors; colleagues staffing the stand.

Constraints

  • Small budget, fixed footprint. A 3×3 m marketplace booth with a defined furniture and print package, and a 2.5 m height limit.
  • Hard external deadlines with financial penalties. Late submissions carried 30–50% surcharges, and 100% on-site.
  • A sensitive category. Legacy planning touches mortality; the conventional startup register of bold claims and urgency would have been actively counterproductive.
  • Very small team. Effectively sole marketing ownership.

Decisions and trade-offs

One idea on the backdrop, not a product summary. The temptation with a small booth is to fit everything onto it. I chose a single legible proposition at a distance over a feature list, accepting that people who wanted detail would have to ask — which was the point, since a question is a conversation.

Calm register over startup urgency. In a hall optimised for attention, I deliberately went quieter. For a product about what happens to your affairs after you die, trust converts and hype repels. The trade-off is real: a calm stand attracts fewer people and better ones.

Front-load the deadlines. All technical service forms were submitted well ahead of the surcharge dates. Unglamorous, but on a small budget a 50% late surcharge is money taken directly out of creative.

Execution

  • Pre-event: objectives and audience definition with founders; booth narrative and creative; vendor selection and negotiation; exhibitor, electrical, furniture and print submissions ahead of deadline.
  • On-site: build supervision and stand set-up; a consistent opening question for everyone staffing the booth so conversations started the same way; qualifying for relevance rather than collecting volume.
  • Post-event: follow-up on the conversations that warranted it.

Outcome

The programme was delivered in full, on time and within budget, with no late surcharges. The company's category proposition was presented to a relevant audience across two days, and the conversations worth continuing were followed up.

The most valuable outcome was not commercial, and it was not the one we went looking for. Two days of face-to-face conversations with the people the product was built for established that the category was not something they actively sought out, and that intent to subscribe over the long term was weak. That is a demand signal, and getting it early — in person, from the actual audience, for the price of a small booth — was worth more to the founders than any volume of badge scans would have been.

Success criteria had been agreed qualitatively rather than commercially, so the programme can be assessed on delivery and on what we learned, but not on return. That is the layer I now fix before committing to an event rather than after it.

What I took from it

An event is a production problem and a commercial programme at the same time. I ran the production side well — scope, vendors, deadlines, build, all delivered. The more valuable lesson was that the commercial side has to be instrumented before anything is booked, because none of it can be retrofitted once the hall is packed down.

The second lesson is the one I would actually argue for. An event is one of the cheapest places to test whether demand exists, and we used it that way almost by accident. If I were designing this again I would make that the explicit brief — go in with the demand hypothesis written down and a structured way to capture what people say — rather than treating validation as a by-product of a stand.

So the checklist I now work through, in order: agree with the business what a successful outcome looks like in commercial terms; build a named list of who we need to meet rather than hoping the right people walk past; define qualification criteria before the doors open; and put the post-event review in the diary before the event, because a review scheduled afterwards never happens.

That sequence is the difference between event marketing and field marketing, and it is the part of the discipline I am most deliberate about now.

Capabilities demonstrated: end-to-end event programme ownership · audience and narrative definition · vendor negotiation and contract management · working to hard external deadlines under financial penalty · cross-functional coordination with founders and suppliers · candid post-mortem
