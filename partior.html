Making a multi-bank settlement network explainable

Rebuilding institutional payments narrative as a modular system, so commercial representatives could match technical depth to the stakeholder in front of them.

Organisation
Partior
My role
Senior Associate, Marketing & Communications — sole marketer
Period
Aug 2025 – present
Audience
Banking and institutional decision-makers

Business context

Partior operates a blockchain-based clearing and settlement platform for global financial institutions, backed by DBS, J.P. Morgan, Standard Chartered and Temasek. The product changes how value moves between banks — which means the people who evaluate it sit across correspondent banking, treasury, operations, technology and risk, and rarely share a common vocabulary.

The problem

The material available to explain the platform had been written from the inside out. It was accurate and thorough, and it assumed the reader already understood how multi-party settlement works. Non-technical decision-makers could not use it to reach a view, which made it a friction point precisely in the conversations that mattered most: time-limited meetings with senior banking stakeholders.

Strategic objective

Give commercial representatives material that establishes the platform's value in the first few minutes of a conversation, and that can go deeper on demand without switching documents or losing the thread.

My precise role

The narrative and its adoption are mine. Conversion is not. As the company's sole marketer — directing an external PR agency — I ran the discovery with internal specialists, made the structural and simplification decisions, built the modular system and produced the video. The bank relationships those assets support are owned by commercial and leadership colleagues, and the decisions they lead to are settled elsewhere.

Stakeholders

Internal product and technical specialists (source of truth on settlement mechanics); commercial and GTM colleagues (the people who would actually use the material); communications legal and finance (accuracy and disclosure review); an external PR agency I direct and set priorities for; leadership, reporting to the CEO. External partner institutions were involved where assets referenced published joint activity.

Constraints

  • Accuracy is non-negotiable. In a regulated multi-bank context, a simplification that becomes misleading is a legal and reputational problem, not just a marketing one.
  • Partner sensitivity. Anything referencing another institution required their position to be represented correctly and, where relevant, approved.
  • Consortium dynamics. Shareholders are also participants, which raises the bar on how the network and its participants are characterised.
  • Small team, wide surface. Narrative, web, content, events and executive material sat with a very small function.

Decisions and trade-offs

Clarity over completeness. The central decision was to simplify technical edge cases so the core value proposition survived a short conversation. This was a real trade-off with a real cost: certain granularities were deliberately left out of the top layer, and specialists could reasonably say the summary was incomplete. I accepted that, because material that is complete but unusable does not help anyone in a thirty-minute meeting.

A system rather than a deck. The alternative — a single beautifully finished narrative — would have been faster to produce and easier to control. I chose a modular structure instead, because a single fixed narrative forces representatives to either overshoot or undershoot their audience, and they will improvise if the material does not fit.

Visual explanation over written explanation. Settlement flows are relational and hard to hold in prose. Committing to a visual-first approach cost more production time and required more review cycles than a written explainer.

Approach and execution

  • Discovery. Worked with internal specialists to break clearing and settlement flows down to first principles, then identified where non-specialist understanding consistently broke down.
  • Modular content system. Built structured narrative flows allowing representatives to select the depth of technical explanation appropriate to each stakeholder, rather than delivering one fixed story. It is now the GTM team's standard material for bank engagements.
  • Flagship visual asset. Produced, in-house and at short notice, a video synthesising a multi-party settlement flow into something a banking audience could follow without prior context — built to coincide with the press release.
  • Review discipline. Every layer of the narrative was validated against product accuracy and reviewed for disclosure and partner sensitivity before release.

What impact means in this category

A marketing asset does not convert a bank. The decision runs through treasury, operations, risk, compliance and technology, over quarters, and is settled by relationship depth, commercial terms and internal risk appetite. Attributing that outcome to a narrative asset would be false precision, and anyone who has sold into this segment knows it.

So I hold a narrower boundary, and measure inside it. What I can be held to is whether the material is accurate, whether commercial teams actually use it, and whether the framing travels — first internally, then outward into the market. Those are leading indicators, and they are genuinely mine.

Outcome

On that measure:

  • Internal adoption. The modular narrative system is now the GTM team's standard material for bank engagements — not an alternative to the existing deck, but the one they use.
  • External adoption — the strongest form available. Deutsche Bank requested the video at short notice, then published it on its own LinkedIn channel and website and displayed it at its Sibos stand. A Tier-1 bank's communications team had to be willing to describe the network in these terms, on their own channels, under their own name.
  • Narrative reach. The core brand narrative was applied across a period recording 131 media mentions.

What I am not claiming: influenced pipeline, accelerated deals or reduced sales-cycle friction. Those outcomes are owned further down the chain, and claiming them would be the easiest way to lose the credibility of everything above.

Evidence

Publicly verifiable: Deutsche Bank — first euro transaction via Partior's blockchain platform (opens in a new tab).

This case study references only material Deutsche Bank and Partior have made public. Internal strategy, commercial terms, unannounced partnerships, customer details and non-public performance data are deliberately excluded.

What I learned

The instinct to be exhaustive is usually the audience's problem, not their solution. The harder skill was not simplifying — it was deciding which inaccuracies of omission were acceptable, and being able to defend those choices to the specialists whose work I was compressing. I also learned that in a consortium structure, review is not an obstacle to route around; the constraint improves the narrative because it forces every claim to be one the company can stand behind.

What I would do differently: instrument adoption properly rather than observing it. I can tell you the GTM team uses the system; I cannot yet tell you which modules get used, where representatives still go off-script, or how long the explain-the-product phase takes now versus before. Attribution to a bank's decision may be out of reach, but that layer is not — and it is the layer I own.
